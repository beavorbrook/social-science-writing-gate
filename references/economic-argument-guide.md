# Economic argument guide — applied, descriptive and environmental economics

This guide is a **new synthesis**, not copied source text. Read it when writing Introduction, Results, Discussion, Conclusions, or performing the independent economics referee gate. Preserve the approved research design.

## A. Choose the correct kind of economic question

1. **What is happening?** Measurement/description: how did city carbon intensity, dispersion or decoupling evolve?
2. **What accounts for an observed aggregate change?** Accounting (Kaya/LMDI): contributions under a chosen identity, not causal mechanisms.
3. **What is associated with it?** Correlational: conditional associations and alternative explanations, no identified causal effect.
4. **Why did it happen?** Causal mechanism: requires discriminating measurements and an identification design, not a literature citation alone.
5. **What should policymakers do?** Decision question: needs policy target, constraints, alternatives, benefits/trade-offs, and evidence about effectiveness; do not infer a unique optimal policy from descriptive facts.

A paper can make a valuable contribution by identifying tensions across 1–3. It should not claim to have answered 4–5 unless evidence permits. It can still discuss relevant theories, competing candidate mechanisms, and **conditional** decision implications.

## B. Results paragraph drafting pattern

Use coherent prose rather than literal labels:

- **Claim:** “All eleven cities lowered fossil CO2 intensity over [period], although their relative positions remained unequal.”
- **Quantitative evidence:** show key city-level magnitudes, economic units, or meaningful benchmark.
- **Interpretive move:** explain what the fact implies about differences between **within-city improvement** and **between-city distribution**, not just how a variance formula works.
- **Alternative/condition:** mention a material boundary or influential city when it alters that substantive judgment.
- **Transition:** raise a concrete economic question the next subsection/discussion can address (e.g., whether sector mix or changes in energy requirements account for persistent rank gaps).

A result is underinterpreted if removing the method name leaves no intelligible fact about an economy.

## C. Discussion paragraph drafting pattern

Structure: **observed fact → economic puzzle → candidate explanation → comparison with source-backed literature → local evidence or lack thereof → competing explanation → meaningful implication**.

Avoid the false choice between “prove a mechanism” and “say nothing economic.” External papers can inform possible channels; a short city panel may support only description. Use both while preserving boundaries. One or two well-chosen mechanisms are better than a list of ten speculative ones.

### Evidence support coding (use in audit matrix)

- **S1 Direct:** directly measured or computed by current study.
- **S2 Study-consistent:** a hypothesis or mechanism is consistent with observable contrasts in current study, no causal identification.
- **S3 External-only:** source publication supports link in another setting; not locally tested.
- **S4 Proposed:** theoretical possibility not yet empirically supported in this project.
- **S5 Contradicted or noncomparable:** evidence conflicts or measures a different phenomenon.

When describing mechanism M:
- State **what M would predict** about economic sector shares, fuel intensity, technology adoption, emissions per unit of energy, or firms' response to incentives.
- Check if a local variable exists that can actually distinguish M from plausible alternative M2.
- If no, use `S3` or `S4`, name the missing dataset, and stop short of attributing causal responsibility.

## D. Worked illustration (Zhejiang carbon intensity paper — explanatory template, NOT a new result)

**Existing fact (S1):** All 11 cities had lower endpoint carbon intensity; city ranks mostly persisted; CV and log-scale spread moved differently. Quzhou had a high starting and ending intensity; Zhoushan exhibited a concentrated 2023–2024 emissions increase in allocated gridded observations.

**Economic question:** How can broad relative efficiency improvement coexist with persistent differences in emissions per unit of output? What prevents relative improvement from ensuring absolute emission reductions?

**Candidate mechanisms (NOT established for Zhejiang):** (i) sectoral composition and specialization; (ii) fuel/energy intensity and energy-system constraints; (iii) technology/innovation uptake; (iv) urbanization and demographics. Describe distinct observable predictions for each. A city dominated by more emissions-intensive economic activity may have higher aggregate intensity even after real improvements; this remains hypothetical until a sector-linked output/emissions comparison is obtained.

**Literature comparison (S3 pending source verification):** A verified study of Chinese urban carbon intensity may report correlates involving industrial structure, innovation, land use or demographic changes. Compare the actual estimated variables, years, spatial units, specification and outcome definition. Do **not** transplant coefficients or assert its mechanism operated in Quzhou. Verify Luo et al. (2025) from the original full article before discussing individual reported mechanisms; its mere citation is not enough.

**LMDI interpretation:** A negative composite-intensity contribution offsets positive output expansion **in the accounting identity**. The aggregate factor does not isolate technology, fuel switching or regulation. More detailed sector-by-fuel information would discriminate channels.

**Policy inference:** Because intensity gains alone may coexist with rising emissions, an assessment that combines **absolute** fossil emissions with intensity provides more information. Differentiated measures could be considered conditional on sector/energy constraints; the present study does not establish a specific intervention's effect or the province's full net-zero pathway.

**A useful discussion sentence in English (illustrative, not an empirical claim):** “Persistent differences in emissions per unit of output may reflect variation in sectoral composition, energy requirements, and the pace of adjustment, channels documented in broader urban studies but not separately identified by the present city-level accounts.”

## E. Counterexamples — what should trigger an economics HOLD?

- “The log IQR increased because it focuses on the middle half” — correct statistical definition, **not an economic explanation**.
- “Removing Quzhou reverses CV” — sample influence, **not the cause of differences between cities**.
- “The LMDI intensity term proves clean technology was successful” — impossible from a composite identity.
- “Three cities are strongly decoupled, so they are on a permanent net-zero path” — endpoint class not longitudinal causal/policy forecast.
- “Technology innovation reduced inequality in Zhejiang” based solely on a national literature citation — unsupported local attribution.
- “Regional cooperation will reduce CO2 by X%” without an intervention estimate — invented policy magnitude.

Correct approach: give the measured fact, explain the economic stakes, discuss calibrated alternative channels and identify what evidence would distinguish them.

## F. A short review for each H1–H4 style finding

- **H1 — Disparities:** distinguish starting positions, proportional improvement and changing sector/energy structure; explain why distributional changes matter for fair comparisons. Avoid claiming formal spatial spillovers from maps alone.
- **H2 — Accounting offsets:** identify the magnitude of output/population/aggregate intensity contributions; ask what further sector/energy evidence would unpack the composite term. Distinguish efficiency improvement from absolute decarbonization.
- **H3 — Decoupling:** economic difference between positive growth + falling emissions versus both still increasing; assess whether the endpoint class is borderline and whether it persists in annual transitions.
- **H4 — Sensitivity:** explain which economic **conclusions** shift (e.g., an individual city's low-carbon ranking), not simply which calculation parameter was changed. Place low-value technical permutations in the appendix.

## H. Ordered review process

(1) Lock paper type and study claims. (2) Identify the main economic puzzle and 1–3 connected claims. (3) Build evidence map. (4) Audit each Results subsection's economic meaning. (5) Audit Discussion for plausible testable explanations and competing accounts. (6) Verify external mechanism sources. (7) Mark claims S1–S5. (8) Perform independent economics referee review. (9) Only then polish prose using two Anti-Defensive passes. (10) Require researcher approval for any new dataset, regression or scope-changing claim.


## I. v2.1 — how to cite an economic explanation (claim-level examples)

**Principle:** One independent major economic judgment calls for directly relevant verified evidence, typically **1–2 studies**. One coherent mechanism cluster may use **1–3 complementary sources**. Source fit and exact placement matter more than citation count; mathematical definitions and original empirical results should rely on the relevant original data rather than gratuitous outside citations.

**Weak and unsupported example (do not use):**

> Industrial specialization, energy structure, technological progress and demographics explain why Quzhou remained carbon-intensive (Shan et al., 2022; Luo et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2020).

This conflates four mechanisms, asserts untested Zhejiang-specific causality and leaves the source-to-proposition mapping unclear.

**Better structure (illustrative; verify original articles before publishing):**

> A plausible explanation concerns the composition of economic activity: an urban economy with a larger output share in emissions-intensive industries may retain a higher aggregate carbon-intensity ratio even as within-sector performance improves. Sectorally disaggregated studies such as Shan et al. (2022) and Zheng and Zheng (2025) help identify the relevant variation and sector boundaries. These studies motivate a possible account of Zhejiang's persistent gaps, but the present aggregate city panel cannot assign the gaps to specific sectors.

> Differences in fuel requirements and efficiency provide a distinct account. Prior city-level evidence should be cited immediately after an accurately summarized finding about technology or energy intensity; the proposition should **not** claim that Zhejiang technology adoption was observed unless local evidence exists. To distinguish this explanation from sector mix would require sector-linked output, fuel or technology measures.

**Source verification reminder:** The above author–year references are worked illustrations, **not authorization to claim their contents have been freshly verified by this Skill**. Read each original paper and record the exact support in the claim-to-source ledger. Do not combine an emissions-inventory data paper, an accounting decomposition and an identified causal paper as if they measure the same mechanism.

**Drafting workflow:** First mark candidate economic claims `[C1]`, `[C2]`, etc. For each, fill in the ledger; remove unsupported sentences or downgrade their level. Then write coherent prose, placing one or two relevant citations around the source-dependent proposition, not all citations at the paragraph's end. Use a short citation cluster only when all its sources jointly support the **same** claim. Finally conduct an independent Cursor sentence-level audit.
